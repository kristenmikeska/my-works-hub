## 12/27 evening · una corda (new event)

Jaehyun went to bed at five to nine. He announced it in the front room doorway like a press conference, laptop under his arm as if somebody might confiscate it, *forty-one to one, I want that on the record,* and went, and they heard his door, and then the apartment did the thing it only ever did with nobody's parents in it, which was nothing at all.

She did the dishes from dinner, which was what was left of the sixty-one dollars and still better than anything that had been cooked in that kitchen in a year, and when she came back down the hall the front room was dark except for the lamps along Fifth coming up off the park, and he was sitting at the piano with the lid up, not playing.

He wasn't doing anything. His hands were on his knees and his head was tipped about two degrees to the left, the way it went when he was listening to something nobody else in a room could hear, and she stood in the doorway and let him, because she'd learned in November that interrupting that was like waking somebody up mid-sentence.

"What are you doing in the dark," she sighs eventually.

"Listening to your house."

"It's an apartment."

"It's a house somebody stacked eleven of," he hums, without turning round. "Come here. Don't turn the light on."

She didn't. She came across the good floor in her socks and he moved over on the bench without looking, half a foot, exactly as much as she'd need, and she sat down in it with her hip against his, on the same bench neither of them had been doing much sitting on the last time they were in this room together, which she was choosing not to think about, and failing.

It was a different room at night. She'd lived in it eighteen years and never once noticed, never once sat in it with the lights off and just listened: the radiators ticking down under the windows, the refrigerator two rooms away, something in the wall that was the elevator going down to Marco, a cab on Fifth with chains on hissing past, the sound coming up eleven floors late and soft, like it had been through a sleeve.

"Heat's off in the hall," he murmurs. "Air's colder out there. Heavier. Everything sits lower at night. Your ceiling's doing about half what it does in the day."

"My ceiling."

"Your ceiling's the best thing in this apartment. Don't tell your father. Twelve feet of old plaster with nothing on it. Somebody built this room a hundred years ago to make a piano sound like money, and then nobody played it."

He put his left foot on the pedal. The left one, which in five years of Mrs. Aristov she'd been told about exactly once and never allowed to touch.

"That's the soft one," she notes.

"Una corda. Shifts the whole action over, so the hammers hit two strings instead of three. Takes the top off everything."

"Why."

"Because your brother's door is forty feet down the hall and round two corners," he shrugs, "and I knocked on every wall between here and there yesterday, and that hall eats everything above about a G. I'm going to play under a G. He's not going to hear a thing."

She looked at him in the dark. He'd knocked on the plaster on the tour, between Jaehyun at nine and Jaehyun at twelve, and she'd thought he was being strange about a hallway. He'd been mapping it.

"You measured my hallway so you could play the piano at night without waking up my brother."

"I measured your hallway because I measure every hallway," he deadpans. "The piano's a bonus."

And then he played.

It was the thing from yesterday. The thing that was *nothing, it's a thing I do.* She knew it by the second bar, and it was different with the soft pedal down, rounder, further off, like hearing it through a door, like somebody on the other side of a wall was playing it for himself and didn't know you were there. He didn't look at her. He played it slow. He got to the place where it lifted, the lift that was the track's lift, the one she'd sung into in 4B on the ninth with headphones on and a click in her ear, and she hummed it.

She didn't decide to. It came out of her the way a hand goes to the railing on a stair, four notes under her breath, barely a sound at all.

He stopped.

Not abruptly. He let the chord he was on go all the way out, and the room took it, and then it was just the radiators again, and his hands were still on the keys.

"Again."

"I wasn't—" she stutters.

"You came in early. You came in on seven. Do it again and come in on nine."

"There isn't a nine, it's not a song, you said it was nothing—"

"It's got a nine. Everything's got a nine," he exhales. "Listen."

He played it again from the top and counted it for her, under his breath, flat, the way he'd counted her into the second *stay* in the booth until her voice went somewhere it had never been, *one, two,* low, not looking at her, just the numbers and his left foot on the soft pedal and the park black in the window behind the piano, *six, seven, eight—*

He stopped on eight and held it, and looked at her, finally, sideways, in the dark.

His phone was on the rug by his foot, face down. Not on the piano. Even he'd worked out in two days that nothing went on the piano. She noticed he hadn't picked it up. She noticed he wasn't going to.

**A. (Come in on nine. Quietly.)** *flag: epi-r-piano-sang*
So she came in on nine. Quietly, under the G, the way you sing in a car with someone asleep in the back seat, four bars of a melody that didn't have words because it had never needed any, over a piano that hadn't had anybody's voice near it since she was eleven years old.
It was nothing. It was the smallest thing she'd ever sung. He played under it like he'd been waiting to, like the part he'd been leaving out yesterday had a shape and the shape had been her the whole time, and when she ran out of melody she just stopped, and he let the last chord go, and neither of them said anything for a long while.
"Yeah," he breathes. "That's where it goes."
"That was nothing."
"That was bar nine."

**B. "Not yet. You can have nine some other time."** *flag: epi-r-piano-saved*
"Okay," he answers, easily.
He didn't push. He never had, on that one. Not since a rooftop in the fall, when he'd asked her to sing for him someday instead of asking for her father, and then left it lying there between them like a key he was never going to pick up for her.
"Nine's yours. It'll keep."
He played the eight bars again, and stopped on eight, and left the ninth empty, and somehow that was louder than anything, the space where she wasn't, sitting in the room like a held breath.

**C. (Put your left hand on the keys under his.)** *flag: epi-r-piano-hands*
Five years of Mrs. Aristov and seven years of nothing, and her left hand went to the keys under his like it had only been gone a weekend. She found the root he'd been sitting on all night, the low thing under everything, and held it. He looked down at her hand, then at her, and took his own left hand off the keyboard entirely and put it on her knee instead, and played the top with his right.
"You said you were fine," he accuses.
"I was fine."
"You're better than fine. You're rushing. But you're better than fine."
Four hands, three of them on a piano. She lasted about eleven bars before she lost count entirely, because of the hand on her knee, which wasn't playing anything and was somehow the loudest thing in the room.

*(all three, after)*

Somewhere down the hall, something clicked. They both froze.

"Radiator," he whispers, after a second. "Your brother's door's a half-tone lower."

She laughed, too loud, straight into his shoulder.

"That was above a G," he notes.

"Shut up."

"That was a B-flat, princess. He's definitely awake now."

"He's not."

"No," he concedes. "He's not."

She reached past him and lowered the fallboard over the keys, slowly, the way her hands still knew, so it didn't knock, and then there was nothing between the two of them and the black lacquer but the dark.

There'd been one rule in this room her entire life. Not a glass, not a plate, not a phone. Nothing on the piano.

He kissed her on the bench with one hand flat on the closed lid behind her, to keep her from leaning back into it, which was so considerate it was almost funny, and then it wasn't funny at all. It was slow, and then it wasn't, the bench too narrow for two people doing what two people were doing on it, her hand fisted in the front of his T-shirt the way it had been yesterday, his thumb finding the strip of skin at her waist where her shirt had ridden up and staying there, warm, like it had been told that was its whole job.

"Your mother's rule," he breathes against her mouth.

"What about it."

"I'm trying very hard to respect it."

"Try harder," she grins.

They kept it, in the end, by a margin she would never describe to anybody. The rug in front of the window was very old and very thick, and the park was black, and the hall, as promised, ate everything above a G.

Down the hall her brother slept through all of it, or was kind enough to pretend he had, which in that family came to the same thing.
